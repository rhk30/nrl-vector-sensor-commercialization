Commercial opportunity ranking

These scores are screening judgments, not audited market-size estimates. They weight patent fit, buyer pull, low-frequency advantage, deployability, and competitive difficulty.

Defense-first wedge

  • Vector sensing can provide directional information in a footprint far smaller than a conventional low-frequency beamforming aperture.
  • The strongest near-term use cases are sonobuoy, distributed ASW sensing, UUV/AUV payloads, and fixed subsea surveillance nodes.
  • Commercialization should likely occur through an OEM / prime / program partner rather than by trying to become a full sonobuoy manufacturer.

Dual-use wedge

  • Directional passive acoustic monitoring for marine mammals and offshore projects.
  • Ports, subsea infrastructure, machinery, and leak/source localization.
  • Oceanographic profiling floats and persistent acoustic observatories.

US 11,287,508 B2

Foundational mesh / floating architecture.

  • Priority date: May 3, 2017.
  • Granted: March 29, 2022.
  • Core claims cover a micro/nano two-dimensional mesh flow meter used for acoustic direction-of-arrival sensing, with multiple deployment embodiments.

Open patent

US 11,408,961 B2

Continuation-in-part / channel-tower architecture.

  • Filed: February 20, 2020.
  • Granted: August 9, 2022.
  • Claims extend into multi-channel viscous-liquid sensing, differently oriented channels, batteries, memory, telemetry, anchors, and detachable/surfacing embodiments.

Open patent

IP interpretation: the defensible package is broader than “a directional hydrophone.” It combines a mesh particle-velocity transducer with deployment and fluid-channel architectures. Other vector-sensor mechanisms exist, so this is not a freedom-to-operate or validity opinion.

Publicly evidenced

  • Fabricated silicon-nitride spider-web prototype.
  • 6 mm outer diameter with roughly 20 μm filament spacing.
  • Approximately 2.7 m of fiber in the published mesh geometry.
  • Measured directional response in air at low frequency.
  • Underwater response modeled in the NRL publication.

Critical diligence gaps

  • Actual in-water sensitivity, bandwidth, self-noise, and bearing error.
  • Flow and tether noise.
  • Biofouling, corrosion, sediment, shock, and long-duration packaging.
  • Readout SWaP and cost.
  • Manufacturing yield and calibration drift.
  • Status of any surviving NRL prototypes or unpublished test data.

Questions to take to NRL / TechLink

  1. Has either architecture ever been tested underwater? Request raw sensitivity, noise-floor, and DOA-error data.
  2. What fabrication masks, process flows, CAD, readout designs, and calibration procedures can transfer?
  3. Are physical prototypes, packaged dies, or test fixtures available?
  4. What were the main technical failure modes during development?
  5. What frequency range and minimum detectable pressure were actually demonstrated in water, if any?
  6. Which programs funded follow-on work after the published prototype?
  7. Can the optical readout be replaced by a lower-SWaP scheme while preserving useful claim coverage?
  8. What rights, data, and technical assistance are available under the current evaluation-license pathway?

90-day de-risking plan

0–30 days Secure evaluation rights, inventor access, technical-data inventory, prototype status, and a claim-level review.

30–60 days Reproduce the published response, characterize readout noise and mesh durability, and document fabrication yield.

60–90 days Run a calibrated underwater test against a reference sensor and measure sensitivity, bearing error, bandwidth, flow sensitivity, and self-noise.
